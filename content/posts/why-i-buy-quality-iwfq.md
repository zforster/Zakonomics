Everyone has an opinion on who wins the AI race. Will it be the chipmakers, the hyperscalers, the model labs, or companies nobody has heard of yet? I don't know. I'm fairly sure nobody else does either.

What I *can* do is own businesses that are likely to come out the other side in good shape, whatever the answer turns out to be. For me, that means a **quality factor index fund**, specifically the iShares Edge MSCI World Quality Factor UCITS ETF, ticker **IWFQ** on the London Stock Exchange.

## What IWFQ actually is

IWFQ tracks the **MSCI World Sector Neutral Quality Index**. It starts from the MSCI World, which covers large and mid-cap companies across 23 developed markets, and scores every company on three things:

1. **Return on equity**: how much profit the business makes on shareholders' money.
2. **Debt to equity**: how much it relies on borrowing.
3. **Earnings variability**: how steady its profits have been over time.

High ROE, low debt and stable earnings score well. The index keeps roughly the top 300 companies by that score. The important bit is that it does this **within each sector**: it picks the best-quality banks from among banks and the best-quality tech firms from among tech firms. It then keeps sector weights close to the parent index's.

It's a rules-based, low-cost fund (the ongoing charge is around 0.25% a year) with no star manager. It just applies a sensible filter to the world's biggest companies.

## Why quality makes sense in the AI age

### 1. I don't have to pick the winner

The internet is a useful comparison. It really did change the world, but plenty of the companies that were meant to win it didn't survive the dot-com crash. The money went to businesses with real profits and the balance sheets to keep investing through the downturn.

A quality screen doesn't try to guess which AI story is right. It asks a duller question: *is this business profitable, low on debt and consistent?* Companies that pass that test are the ones best able to fund AI spending from their own cash, and to survive if the hype cools.

### 2. AI is expensive, and the screen watches how it's paid for

Building AI infrastructure means enormous capital spending on data centres, chips and power. Some of it is paid for from operating cash flow. More and more is paid for with debt.

That's where the leverage measure earns its keep. A company that borrows heavily to chase AI will see its debt-to-equity rise and its quality score fall, so the index gradually holds less of it. I don't have to watch every bond issue myself; the rules do it at each rebalance.

### 3. The gains won't only go to tech

The biggest long-run payoff from AI may come from *using* it rather than building it: insurers cutting claims costs, industrial firms improving logistics, healthcare companies speeding up research. Because IWFQ is sector neutral, I own the highest-quality names across *every* sector, not just the tech firms making the headlines.

High-ROE businesses usually have something that protects their margins, such as a brand, a network, switching costs or scale. When AI cuts their costs, those businesses are more likely to keep the savings as profit instead of having them competed away. That's the kind of company I want to own when a new technology spreads.

### 4. If it's a bubble, quality has tended to cushion the fall

I'm not calling a bubble. But if AI valuations do correct, I'd rather hold profitable, low-debt companies than the most speculative end of the market. Historically, the quality factor has tended to hold up better than the broad market in sell-offs. That's not guaranteed, but it's a sensible tilt when valuations are stretched and sentiment runs hot.

### 5. I still get the AI upside

This isn't a bet *against* AI. Sector neutrality means IWFQ still has a large weight in information technology, which is around 40% of the fund at the time of writing. Many of the profitable, cash-generating tech leaders pass the quality screen easily. I keep meaningful exposure to the AI build-out, just filtered for financial strength.

## The risks I accept

No fund is free of risk, and quality has some specific ones:

- **It looks backwards.** ROE and earnings stability are measured on past results. AI could erode the moat of a business that looks excellent on today's numbers; software and IT services firms are the obvious candidates.
- **It can lag in speculative rallies.** When unprofitable, story-driven stocks surge, a quality fund will trail. In a full-blown AI mania, that could last a while.
- **It's still concentrated in tech.** Sector neutrality limits the tilt but doesn't remove the market's own heavy weighting to technology, and especially to US technology.
- **Currency.** The underlying holdings are mostly in US dollars, so for a UK investor the sterling return also depends on exchange rates.
- **Factor cycles.** No factor beats the market every year. There will be stretches when a plain global tracker does better.

## The bottom line

I don't know which company will dominate AI. I do know I want to own businesses that earn high returns on capital, don't depend on debt and have a record of steady profits, and I want to own them across the whole economy at low cost. IWFQ does that for me, on a set of rules I understand.

If AI turns out to be as big as the optimists think, quality companies are well placed to benefit. If it disappoints, they're well placed to survive. I'm comfortable with either outcome.
