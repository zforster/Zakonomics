In my early twenties, while at university, I worked a part time contracting role for a large UK based asset manager. It was through this employement that I became an auto-enrolled member of Nest pensions; the UK's largest workplace pension scheme.

Recently, I browsed the various funds that Nest provide; intrigued at how their offering had changed in since I last evaluated the options and decided to switch out of the default. Sadly, I have bad news for those seeking an all-equity portfolio as **Nest offers its members no equity only option**.

In my opinion Nest needs to address this lack of choice. It particuarly harms those engaged younger investors like myself who understand risk, their investment time horizon and the benefits of long term equity ownership. I believe the lack of an equity only offering is against the long term interests of Nests' members, particuarly those who want agency over their portfolio and are seeking to maximise their savings in retirement.

### What Is a Nest Pension?

Nest is an attractive option for UK based employers, who have a legal obligation to contribute to an employee's pension. Being free to setup with minimal adminstrative effort, it's no wonder more than 500,000 employers have chosen Nest to provide their workplace pension. Nest now manages a hefty £55 billion on behalf of its membership.

### Examining Nests' Fund Options

Nest has 7 funds in total, although 2 of these are targetted to those who are ready to retire. For those of working age, there are 5 major funds that Nest offers.

- Nest Retirement Date Fund (Default)
- Nest Ethical Fund
- Nest Sharia Fund
- Nest Higher Risk Fund
- Nest Lower Growth Fund

### My Nest Fund Choice

I consider myself to be financially literate and able to make sound investment decisions on my own behalf. With all my workplace pensions I have switched out of the typically risk-off default strategy and pivoted my investments into a low cost global equity tracker, seeking to maximise returns over a long time horizon while ignoring short term volatility.

When doing this with Nest, I recall browsing through the fund options and being suprised at the lack of choice for those seeking to be fully risk on in equities. While the _Nest Higher Risk Fund_ holds the majority of its assets in developed and emerging market public equities, it holds a significant amount in junk bonds, private equity, property and cash.

At the time, the only all equity fund available was the _Nest Sharia Fund_. This fund exists to allow the UK's muslim population to invest into a pension while holding Sharia compliant assets. It applies exclusions to companies that profit from alcohol, adult entertainment or pork products, also excluding interest paying assets such as bonds. I swtiched out of the default into the Sharia fund, not for any religious purposes, but because I wanted full exposure to equities and this was the closest I could get, and accepted the reduced company diversification caused by the exclusions.

### Changes to The Fund

The previously all equity _Nest Sharia Fund_ has had its allocation of equities reduced to a fixed 70%, with a 30% allocation to a Sharia compliant product similar to bonds.

Paul Todd, the COO of Nest Invest justified this change on the excellent [_Making Money Podcast_](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-YFfugfvuGM), putting forward the case that the Sharia fund was designed to provide a place for Muslim investors to save for retirement. Nest determined that for those investors reaching retirement age, holding all of their portfolio in equities wasn't desirable. They therefore took the decision to de-risk some of the portfolio in the interest of their members. The Sharia fund was never designed to be 100% equity, this was simply a reflection of the investable universe Nest had when creating the fund. As Sharia compliant products have developed in sophistiacation, they can now offer a product they feel is better suited to its members needs.
