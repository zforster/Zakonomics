> **Note:** this is a write-up of my own thinking, not financial advice. Figures are from the latest factsheets and holdings data available in October 2026. Check them yourself before buying anything.

For years my core holding was **Fundsmith Equity**. Terry Smith's pitch is about as simple as investing gets: *buy good companies, don't overpay, do nothing.* I bought into that completely, and I still do.

What changed is *how* I get that exposure. These days I hold the **iShares Edge MSCI World Quality Factor ETF (IWFQ)** instead. It's built on the same core idea, owning profitable, financially strong businesses, but it does it by rules rather than by one person's judgement, at about a quarter of the cost.

This post explains why I think quality is worth owning, why I moved away from Fundsmith, and what IWFQ quietly keeps out of my portfolio.

## What "quality" means

A quality company earns high returns on the money invested in it, doesn't depend on borrowing, and produces steady profits year after year. Think Visa, Microsoft or a big pharmaceutical company, rather than a cash-burning start-up or a cyclical miner.

The case for owning them isn't just intuition. In their well-known paper [*Quality Minus Junk*](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2312432), Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen found that profitable, safe, well-run companies have delivered higher risk-adjusted returns across 24 countries. The odd part is that the market doesn't charge much extra for them. And because investors pile into quality when things go wrong, these stocks have tended to hold up better in crises.

## What the numbers say

The index IWFQ tracks, the MSCI World Sector Neutral Quality Index, has data going back to 1998. Here's how it compares with the plain MSCI World (US dollars, to 30 September 2026):

| | Quality index | MSCI World |
|---|---|---|
| Annual return since Nov 1998 | **8.14%** | 7.38% |
| Worst fall (Oct 2007 – Mar 2009) | **−54.5%** | −57.8% |
| Return per unit of risk (Sharpe ratio) | **0.45** | 0.39 |
| Sensitivity to the market (beta) | **0.95** | 1.00 |

An extra 0.8% a year doesn't sound like much. Compounded over 28 years, it adds up to a lot, and it came with slightly *smaller* falls along the way. In 2018, for example, the quality index fell 7.2% while the market fell 8.7%.

It's not a magic shield, though. In 2022 the quality index fell 19.2%, slightly *more* than the market's 18.1%, because rising interest rates hit expensive tech and growth stocks hard. And the early years are back-tested, because the index only launched in 2014. The edge is real but modest.

## Why I left Fundsmith

To be clear, Fundsmith has been a brilliant fund. Since launching in 2010 it has returned around 590%, against about 523% for the MSCI World. I don't regret owning it. But over time a few things bothered me.

**1. The cost.** Fundsmith's ongoing charge is about **1.05% a year**. IWFQ's is about **0.25%**. On a £100,000 portfolio, that's £1,050 a year against £250, every year, compounding against me.

**2. Five years of lagging.** 2025 was Fundsmith's fifth calendar year in a row behind the MSCI World. In the first half of 2026 it fell 2.9% while the index rose, a 14-point gap. Every fund has bad patches, but paying active fees for index-lagging returns gets harder to justify.

**3. One person.** Fundsmith's process is closely tied to Terry Smith himself. That's a strength when he's right, and a single point of failure otherwise. IWFQ doesn't depend on anyone's judgement, health or retirement plans.

**4. Concentration.** Fundsmith holds a small number of stocks, around 25–30, so one or two mistakes matter a lot. IWFQ holds nearly 300 quality companies across every sector and 23 countries.

**5. I could get most of the idea for less.** Fundsmith's first rule, *buy good companies*, is essentially a quality screen. A rules-based index can do that part well. What it can't do is Smith's second rule, *don't overpay*. I'm accepting that trade-off in exchange for lower costs and no key-person risk.

## How IWFQ picks its companies

The index starts with the roughly 1,300 large and mid-sized companies in the MSCI World and scores each on three measures:

1. **Return on equity**: how much profit it makes on shareholders' money.
2. **Debt to equity**: how much it relies on borrowing.
3. **Earnings variability**: how steady its profits have been.

It keeps the best-scoring companies *within each sector*, so it doesn't simply turn into a tech fund. Weights are a mix of company size and quality score, and it rebalances twice a year.

The top holdings today look a lot like a Terry Smith wish list: **Microsoft, Apple, Nvidia, Meta, Visa, ASML, Eli Lilly, Mastercard**, plus steady compounders like TJX, Costco and Caterpillar.

## What it keeps out, and why that might matter

Just as important as what a quality fund owns is what it *doesn't*.

Right now the clearest example is the AI build-out. Some companies are funding enormous data centre projects with debt. **Oracle** is the extreme case: negative free cash flow of $23.7 billion last year, well over $100 billion of debt and a debt-to-equity ratio above 4x. Oracle isn't in IWFQ. Nor are Broadcom, still carrying about $55 billion of net debt from its VMware deal, or companies with erratic profits like Tesla, Micron and Palantir.

Together with Amazon, AMD and Intel, those names make up almost **13% of the S&P 500**, and none of IWFQ. They're excluded for different reasons, some for debt and some for unreliable earnings or weak returns, but they share one trait: they're the kind of companies that tend to fall hardest when sentiment turns.

If the AI boom ends in a sharp sell-off, I'll still lose money. IWFQ owns plenty of tech, and the leaders like Nvidia and Microsoft would fall too. But I'd expect to be *slightly* better protected, because the most indebted and speculative names aren't in my portfolio. That's the same pattern as 2008 and 2018: not immunity, just a softer landing.

The screen isn't perfect. Meta is one of IWFQ's biggest holdings despite borrowing heavily for AI, partly because some of that financing sits off its balance sheet where the screen can't see it. Debt-to-equity is a blunt tool. But blunt is still better than nothing.

## The trade-offs

- **It lags in speculative rallies.** The quality index trailed the market in 2024 and 2025. When the most exciting stocks are flying, a quality fund will look boring.
- **Quality isn't cheap.** IWFQ's holdings trade at about 6.7x book value, against 4.1x for the MSCI World. This is exactly the "don't overpay" problem Fundsmith tries to manage and an index doesn't.
- **Not every crash.** In 2022 it fell more than the market. Rising rates hurt quality stocks because many of them are priced on long-term growth.
- **Currency.** Most holdings are in dollars, so as a UK investor my returns also depend on the pound.

## The bottom line

I still believe in what Terry Smith taught me: own great businesses and leave them alone. IWFQ lets me do that across almost 300 companies, with no dependence on one manager, for a quarter of the price.

It won't win every year, and it won't make me immune to a crash. But it keeps me in profitable, low-debt businesses and out of the most fragile corners of the market. And it does that cheaply enough that the fees aren't eating my returns.
