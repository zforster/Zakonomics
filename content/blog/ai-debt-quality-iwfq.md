For most of the last decade, Big Tech paid for its ambitions out of its own pocket. Huge cash flows funded the data centres, and the balance sheets stayed pristine.

That's changing. The AI build-out is so expensive that the biggest names are now borrowing to keep up. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle sold roughly **$220 billion of bonds in 2026 by mid-August**, against around $750 billion of expected capital spending this year. Goldman Sachs expects about a third of hyperscaler capex to be debt-funded.

I'm not saying the spending is wrong. I am saying I'd rather not own the companies doing the most borrowing. That's a big part of why my core holding is the **iShares Edge MSCI World Quality Factor ETF (IWFQ)** rather than a plain S&P 500 tracker.

## Oracle: the poster child

Oracle is the clearest example of an AI bet funded with debt.

- **Free cash flow was negative $23.7 billion** in its 2026 financial year, according to Oracle's own results. It's spending far more on AI infrastructure than it brings in.
- **Total debt** has climbed to somewhere between $125 billion and $170 billion, depending on what you count (leases make the difference). That's up from about $100 billion in late 2024.
- **Debt-to-equity is above 4x** by most estimates. For comparison, Microsoft's is a fraction of that.
- It raised around **$43 billion in new debt** in its 2026 financial year. Its credit rating sits at the bottom of investment grade (BBB) with a negative outlook, and analysts have openly discussed the risk of a cut to junk.

Oracle might pull it off. It has a huge order backlog from AI labs. But a company that borrows heavily to build data centres for a handful of customers is exactly the kind of business a quality screen is designed to avoid. And it does: **Oracle is not in IWFQ at all.**

## How the screen works

IWFQ tracks the **MSCI World Sector Neutral Quality Index**. It scores every company in the MSCI World on three equally weighted measures:

1. **Return on equity**: how much profit it makes on shareholders' money.
2. **Debt to equity**: how much it relies on borrowing.
3. **Earnings variability**: how steady its profits have been.

It keeps roughly the top 300, picks them *within each sector*, and weights them by a mix of size and quality score. On 13 October it held 289 companies. Only 133 of the S&P 500's 504 holdings made the cut.

## What's missing compared with the S&P 500

None of these big AI-related S&P 500 names are in IWFQ. The index doesn't publish each company's score, so the reasons are my reading of which of the three measures each one falls down on:

| Company | S&P 500 weight | Likely reason |
|---|---|---|
| Amazon | 3.77% | Volatile profits (a loss in 2022) and heavy borrowing for capex |
| Broadcom | 2.53% | Debt from the ~$69bn VMware deal; net debt still ~$55bn |
| Micron | 1.60% | Boom-and-bust memory cycle means highly variable earnings |
| Tesla | 1.57% | Profits have swung sharply and fallen from their peak |
| AMD | 1.47% | Low return on equity, diluted by the Xilinx acquisition |
| Intel | 0.76% | Recent heavy losses |
| Palantir | 0.71% | Short, volatile history of profitability |
| Oracle | 0.37% | Debt over 4x equity, negative free cash flow |
| **Total** | **12.78%** | |

That's almost **13% of the S&P 500** in eight AI-linked companies that IWFQ won't touch. Only some of them fail on debt. Amazon, Tesla, Micron and Palantir are mostly excluded for erratic earnings, and AMD and Intel for weak returns. The screen isn't anti-AI. It's anti-fragile-balance-sheet and anti-unreliable-profits.

Further down the market, the same pattern holds. Data centre landlords, the new AI power stocks and newer cloud providers are all absent. CoreWeave, which isn't in the S&P 500, is the extreme case: around $30 billion of debt, deeply negative cash flow and junk-rated bonds paying a 9.75% coupon.

The fund still owns the AI leaders with fortress balance sheets: Microsoft, Nvidia, Apple and Alphabet. It also leans into the "picks and shovels" businesses with exceptional returns on capital. **ASML, Lam Research, Applied Materials, KLA and Advantest** make up about 8% of IWFQ. They sell the equipment every chipmaker needs, whoever wins the AI race.

The rest goes to steady compounders the S&P 500 under-weights: **Visa (3.7%), Mastercard (2.1%), Eli Lilly, TJX, Caterpillar, Costco**. And because it's a global fund (about 74% US), it adds European and Japanese quality names that an S&P 500 tracker can't hold.

## Where the screen falls short

It's not perfect, and the Meta example shows why.

Meta is *over*-weighted in IWFQ, at 4.35% against 2.50% in the S&P 500, despite selling a record **$30 billion bond in October 2025**. Alongside it, Meta arranged roughly **$27 billion of off-balance-sheet financing** for its Hyperion data centre in Louisiana. Debt kept off the balance sheet doesn't show up in debt-to-equity, so the screen can't see it. Meta's enormous equity base also means even large borrowing barely moves the ratio.

The screen also looks backwards. It uses reported accounts and rebalances twice a year, so a company that starts borrowing heavily today won't drop out until the numbers catch up.

So IWFQ isn't a perfect filter for AI debt. It's a blunt one that catches the worst cases, like Oracle, and leaves out a lot of the fragile, cyclical and speculative end of the trade.

## The trade-off

There are costs:

- **You miss rallies.** If Oracle, Broadcom, AMD or Palantir have huge years, an S&P 500 tracker gets them and IWFQ doesn't.
- **It's still concentrated.** The top 10 holdings are 37% of the fund, close to the S&P 500's 39%. It's concentrated differently, not less.
- **It costs more.** The ongoing charge is around 0.25% a year, against under 0.1% for many S&P 500 trackers.
- **Currency.** The holdings are mostly in dollars, so UK investors also take on exchange-rate risk.

## The bottom line

The AI boom is now partly running on credit. Some of that borrowing will turn out brilliantly. Some won't. I don't know which, and I don't have to guess.

By owning IWFQ, I keep the AI leaders that fund their spending from profits, and I avoid the ones betting their balance sheets on it. If the build-out pays off, I still benefit. If it ends in a credit hangover, I've largely sat out the companies most exposed to it.
