> **Note:** this is a write-up of my own thinking, not financial advice. Figures are from the latest factsheets and holdings data available in October 2026. Check them yourself before buying anything.

If I had to sum up my investing in one sentence, it would be: *own good businesses and leave them alone.*

That idea has a name, **quality factor investing**. It's the core of my portfolio, through the **iShares Edge MSCI World Quality Factor ETF (IWFQ)**. This post explains what quality is, what the evidence says, why I think it matters more than usual during the AI boom, and what it protects me from (a little) when markets turn.

## What "quality" means

A quality company earns high returns on the money invested in it, doesn't depend on borrowing, and produces steady profits year after year. Think Visa, Microsoft or a big pharmaceutical company, rather than a cash-burning start-up or a cyclical miner.

The case for owning them isn't just intuition. In their well-known paper [*Quality Minus Junk*](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2312432), Asness, Frazzini and Pedersen found that profitable, safe, well-run companies have delivered higher risk-adjusted returns across 24 countries. The odd part is that the market doesn't charge much extra for them. And because investors pile into quality when things go wrong, these stocks have tended to hold up better in crises.

## What the numbers say

The index IWFQ tracks, the MSCI World Sector Neutral Quality Index, has data going back to 1998. Here's how it compares with the plain MSCI World (US dollars, to 30 September 2026):

| | Quality index | MSCI World |
|---|---|---|
| Annual return since Nov 1998 | **8.14%** | 7.38% |
| Worst fall (Oct 2007 – Mar 2009) | **−54.5%** | −57.8% |
| Return per unit of risk (Sharpe ratio) | **0.45** | 0.39 |
| Sensitivity to the market (beta) | **0.95** | 1.00 |

An extra 0.8% a year doesn't sound like much. Compounded over 28 years, it adds up to a lot, and it came with slightly *smaller* falls along the way. In 2018, for example, the quality index fell 7.2% while the market fell 8.7%.

It's not a magic shield, though. In 2022 the quality index fell 19.2%, slightly *more* than the market's 18.1%, because rising interest rates hit expensive tech and growth stocks hard. And the early years are back-tested, because the index only launched in 2014. The edge is real but modest.

## How IWFQ picks its companies

The index starts with the roughly 1,300 large and mid-sized companies in the MSCI World and scores each on three measures:

1. **Return on equity**: how much profit it makes on shareholders' money.
2. **Debt to equity**: how much it relies on borrowing.
3. **Earnings variability**: how steady its profits have been.

It keeps the best-scoring companies *within each sector*, so it doesn't simply turn into a tech fund. Weights are a mix of company size and quality score, and it rebalances twice a year. It's cheap, too: about **0.25% a year**, with no star manager to pay or to depend on.

## Why quality matters more in the AI boom

For most of the last decade, Big Tech paid for its ambitions out of its own pocket. That's changing. The AI build-out is so expensive that the biggest names are now borrowing to keep up. Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle sold roughly **$220 billion of bonds in 2026 by mid-August**, and Goldman Sachs expects about a third of hyperscaler spending to be debt-funded.

That matters for a quality investor, because two of the three measures above, **debt** and **earnings stability**, are exactly what an AI spending race puts under strain.

**Oracle** is the extreme case. Its free cash flow was **negative $23.7 billion** in its 2026 financial year. Its debt has climbed to well over $100 billion, more than 4x its equity, and its credit rating sits at the bottom of investment grade with a negative outlook. Oracle might pull it off. But a company borrowing heavily to build data centres for a handful of customers is exactly what a quality screen is designed to avoid, and **Oracle isn't in IWFQ.**

## What IWFQ leaves out

Comparing IWFQ's holdings with the S&P 500 on 13 October 2026, these AI-linked names are in the S&P 500 but not in IWFQ:

| Company | S&P 500 weight | Likely reason |
|---|---|---|
| Amazon | 3.77% | Volatile profits (a loss in 2022) and heavy borrowing for capex |
| Broadcom | 2.53% | Debt from the ~$69bn VMware deal; net debt still ~$55bn |
| Micron | 1.60% | Boom-and-bust memory cycle means highly variable earnings |
| Tesla | 1.57% | Profits have swung sharply and fallen from their peak |
| AMD | 1.47% | Low return on equity, diluted by the Xilinx acquisition |
| Intel | 0.76% | Recent heavy losses |
| Palantir | 0.71% | Short, volatile history of profitability |
| Oracle | 0.37% | Debt over 4x equity, negative free cash flow |
| **Total** | **12.78%** | |

MSCI doesn't publish individual scores, so the reasons are my reading of where each one falls down. Only some fail on debt. Others fail on erratic earnings or weak returns. The screen isn't anti-AI. It's anti-fragile-balance-sheet and anti-unreliable-profits.

IWFQ still owns plenty of AI, just the financially strongest end of it. The "Magnificent Seven" make up **35%** of the S&P 500 but **25%** of IWFQ:

| Amazon | 3.77% | — |
| Meta | 2.50% | 4.35% |

It also leans into the "picks and shovels" businesses with exceptional returns on capital. **ASML, Lam Research, Applied Materials, KLA and Advantest** make up about 8% of the fund. They sell the equipment every chipmaker needs, whoever wins the AI race.

The rest goes to steady compounders like **Visa, Mastercard, Eli Lilly, TJX, Costco and Caterpillar**. And because it's a global fund (about 74% US), it adds European and Japanese quality names an S&P 500 tracker can't hold.

## If the AI boom turns to bust

If AI valuations crash, I'll still lose money. IWFQ owns plenty of tech, and leaders like Nvidia and Microsoft would fall too. But I'd expect to be *slightly* better protected, because the most indebted and speculative names aren't in my portfolio. That's the same pattern as 2008 and 2018: not immunity, just a softer landing.

The screen isn't perfect, and Meta shows why. It's *over*-weighted in IWFQ despite selling a record **$30 billion bond in October 2025**, partly because around **$27 billion** of its data centre financing sits off its balance sheet, where debt-to-equity can't see it. The screen also uses reported accounts and rebalances only twice a year, so a company that starts borrowing heavily today won't drop out until the numbers catch up. It's a blunt filter, but it catches the worst cases.

## The trade-offs

- **It lags in speculative rallies.** The quality index trailed the market in 2024 and 2025. When the most exciting stocks are flying, a quality fund will look boring.
- **Quality isn't cheap.** IWFQ's holdings trade at about 6.7x book value, against 4.1x for the MSCI World. You're paying up for good businesses, and an index can't avoid overpaying.
- **Not every crash.** In 2022 it fell more than the market. Rising rates hurt quality stocks because many of them are priced on long-term growth.
- **Still concentrated.** The top 10 holdings are 37% of the fund, close to the S&P 500's 39%. It's concentrated differently, not less.
- **It costs more than a plain tracker.** 0.25% a year is cheap, but many global and S&P 500 trackers charge under 0.1%.
- **Currency.** Most holdings are in dollars, so as a UK investor my returns also depend on the pound.

## The bottom line

Quality investing comes down to a simple idea: own great businesses and leave them alone. IWFQ lets me do that across almost 300 companies, cheaply and by clear rules.

The AI boom makes that discipline more useful, not less. Some of the borrowing will pay off brilliantly, and some won't. I don't have to guess which. By owning quality, I keep the AI leaders that fund their spending from profits and avoid most of the ones betting their balance sheets on it. If the build-out pays off, I still benefit. If it ends in a hangover, I've largely sat out the companies most exposed to it.
